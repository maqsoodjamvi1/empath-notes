Walking into the breakroom at work, you feel totally fine. Two minutes later, your chest is tight, your jaw is clamped shut, and you are seething with a sudden, irrational urge to snap at the next person who speaks to you.

If you identify as a Highly Sensitive Person (HSP) or an empath, this probably sounds familiar. You assume you absorbed someone else’s rage like a human sponge. But are you actually feeling their anger, or are you just intensely overstimulated by the flickering fluorescent lights, the hum of the refrigerator, and the background chatter?

When your nervous system is dialed up to eleven, it stops being able to process inputs accurately. It triggers a fight-or-flight response, and that physical panic often masquerades as anger—yours or someone else’s. Making the wrong call here leads you to spend hours shielding against negative psychic energy when you really just needed to take off your headphones, drink a glass of water, and sit in a dark room for ten minutes.

Here is a practical decision guide to help you figure out what is actually happening in your body and mind.

Run the Physical Environment Audit First

Before you start analyzing the emotional climate of the room, you have to check your basic biological inputs. HSPs process sensory data deeply, meaning minor annoyances pile up quickly into a massive nervous system traffic jam.

Ask yourself these three questions immediately when a sudden mood shift hits:

  • When was the last time I ate a protein-rich meal or drank water? (Low blood sugar mimics irritability and rage with terrifying precision.)
  • What does my body actually feel like right now? Do I have a headache, tight shoulders, or cold hands?
  • What is happening in the room sensorially? Is there a high-pitched whine from an appliance, or are three different conversations happening at once?

If you check three or more boxes for physical depletion or sensory overload, stop looking for an emotional culprit. You aren’t picking up on a coworker’s secret hostility; your nervous system is simply redlining from too much data.

Look for the “Target” in the Feeling

If your physical environment checks out and you are still drowning in a wave of hostility, it is time to look at the shape of the emotion itself. Genuine energetic absorption behaves differently than personal overstimulation or standard stress.

True emotional contagion—absorbing someone else’s anger—usually arrives with a specific flavor. It feels foreign, like wearing a sweater that belongs to someone else. It often comes with mental images or thoughts that do not match your current life situation. For example, if you are happily working on a spreadsheet and suddenly experience a sharp spike of fury about someone not doing the dishes, but your own kitchen is clean, you are likely feeling someone else’s domestic frustration.

On the other hand, overstimulation anger is broad, dull, and heavy. It targets everything and everyone equally. If you are mad at the way your pen clicks, the way your cat breathes, and the state of the global economy all at once, that is pure nervous system burnout. You aren’t feeling someone else’s specific anger; you are feeling your own brain screaming for a break.

Use the Spatial Test

Distance is the ultimate diagnostic tool for empaths. If you suspect you have absorbed someone else’s bad mood, change your location drastically.

Do not just walk to the other side of the room; leave the building entirely, or go sit in your car for five minutes. Roll the windows down, close your eyes, and take slow belly breaths.

Monitor what happens to the anger during this break:

  • If it belongs to the environment: The rage will begin to lift or soften within three to five minutes of physical removal and quiet. As your nervous system recalibrates, the foreign feeling evaporates because the source is gone.
  • If it belongs to you (overstimulation or internalized stress): The anger might morph into exhaustion, sadness, or a headache, but it won’t just vanish. You will still feel wrung out because your own battery is dead.

Build a Personal Protocol for the Next Wave

Distinguishing between overstimulation and emotional absorption gets easier the more you track your patterns. Keep a simple note on your phone for a week. Every time you feel a sudden surge of anger, write down your physical state, the noise level around you, and whether the feeling stayed or left when you moved away.

Understanding the root cause of your sudden mood shifts saves you from wasting energy trying to clear or protect yourself from “bad vibes” when what you really need is a snack, silence, and a boundary. As you figure out how your specific system reacts to stress, deciding your next move becomes much clearer and less exhausting.
