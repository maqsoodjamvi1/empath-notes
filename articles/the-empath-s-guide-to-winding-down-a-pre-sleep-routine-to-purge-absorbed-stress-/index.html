If you are an empath, bedtime often does not feel like a relief. Instead, it feels like the moment the day’s debris finally catches up with you. You lie down in a dark room, close your eyes, and suddenly realize your heart is racing, your lower back is tight, and you are ruminating over a coworker’s passive-aggressive sigh from nine hours ago or a stranger’s visible distress on the subway.

For years, I thought I just had terrible insomnia. I tried cutting out caffeine, buying expensive blackout curtains, and listening to white noise. None of it worked because the root cause wasn’t physical—it was energetic. I was trying to sleep while carrying three other people’s emotional baggage in my nervous system.

To actually fall asleep, I had to stop treating bedtime like a simple physical shutdown and start treating it like a decontamination process. Here is the exact pre-sleep routine I use to purge absorbed stress and reclaim my nights.

The 9:00 PM Energy Audit

The purging process has to start before your head hits the pillow. If you wait until you are under the covers to process the day, your brain will use that quiet time to replay every interaction you absorbed.

At least an hour before sleep, I sit down with a physical notebook and do what I call an energy audit. I ask myself two specific questions: What emotions am I currently feeling? and Whose are they?

As an empath, it is entirely too easy to take on the anxiety of a partner, the overwhelm of a client, or the collective dread of a news cycle and mistake them for my own. Write them down without editing. If you feel a sudden wave of panic about money, but your bank account is fine, write down: “Panic—belongs to my boss.” If you feel heavy grief that came out of nowhere, write down: “Grief—belongs to the friend I texted earlier.”

Naming the emotion and assigning it back to its rightful owner creates a psychological boundary. It tells your nervous system, “This is not my backpack to carry.” Once it’s on paper, physically close the notebook. That action signals to your brain that the sorting phase is over.

The Physical Shedding Ritual

Emotions do not just live in our heads; they settle directly into our fascia and muscles. When we absorb stress, we tend to hold it in our jaw, shoulders, and hips. Before I get into bed, I spend three minutes physically shaking the day out of my body.

Stand up with your feet hip-width apart and soft knees. Start shaking your hands vigorously, as if you are trying to flick water off your fingertips. Let the movement travel up into your arms, shoulders, and torso. Bounce gently on your heels.

It looks ridiculous, but animals do this instinctively after surviving a threat—they literally shake off the adrenaline. As you shake, consciously exhale through your mouth with an audible sigh. Imagine every heavy, foreign emotion you picked up at the grocery store, online, or at work sliding off your skin and falling onto the floor.

The Salt-Water Cleansing Shower

If my day was particularly heavy—say, I spent an hour listening to a friend’s marital crisis or navigated a toxic office environment—shaking isn’t always enough. I need a hard reset.

Water is a powerful psychological and physical transition tool for empaths. Before getting into bed, I take a quick, lukewarm shower, but with a specific protocol:

  • I use a handful of plain Epsom salt or sea salt as a body scrub, starting at my neck and working down to my feet.
  • As I scrub, I visualize the salt acting like a microscopic comb, catching all the emotional static and energetic residue clinging to my aura.
  • When I rinse off, I watch the water swirl down the drain and mentally state, “I am washing off what is not mine.”

Stepping out of that shower feels like stepping out of someone else’s wet coat. Your skin feels your own again.

Setting the Nighttime Boundary

Once you get into bed, you need to protect the space you just cleared. Empaths are notoriously porous, and many of us sleep with our emotional antennas fully extended, accidentally tuning into the dreams or anxieties of people in the next room—or even halfway across the world.

Before I pull up the blankets, I set a firm mental and energetic boundary for the night. I literally tell myself out loud: “My energetic field is closed for the night. I release responsibility for everyone else’s well-being until morning.”

If you share a bed with a partner who snores, tosses, or also carries heavy stress, put a physical barrier between your sides—an extra pillow down the middle can act as an energetic buffer while you sleep.

Transitioning from a sponge that absorbs everything to a person who rests peacefully takes practice. You might find that your mind wanders back to someone else’s problems in the middle of the night; if it does, gently remind yourself that you cannot help them if you are running on empty. Taking these steps to clear your slate ensures that when you finally close your eyes, you are resting as you, not as a storage unit for the world.
